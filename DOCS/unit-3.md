Lesson 7: Types of Radiation — Ionizing vs Non-Ionizing
Title: Types of Radiation — Ionizing vs Non-Ionizing
Description: Introduce learners to the electromagnetic spectrum and nuclear radiation types (alpha, beta, gamma, neutron). Explain the difference between ionizing and non-ionizing radiation and why not all radiation is harmful.
Unit: Unit 3 — Radiation & Its Effects
Difficulty: Beginner
Prerequisite: Basic knowledge of atomic structure and nuclear reactions (Units 1 & 2)
Default Steps
1. BRIEFING - Mission Intro
“Agent, radiation is all around you, from sunlight to medical X-rays. Your mission: identify the different types of radiation and understand which are safe and which can be harmful.”
2. MYTH BUSTER - Bust a misconception
Myth: “All radiation is deadly.”
Truth: Radiation comes in many forms. Non-ionizing radiation (e.g., radio waves, microwaves, visible light) is generally safe, while ionizing radiation (e.g., alpha, beta, gamma, neutrons) has enough energy to affect atoms and molecules. Safety depends on exposure type and dose.
3. MICRO LEARN - Core teaching content
A. What Is Radiation?
Energy traveling through space as:
Waves (electromagnetic radiation)


Particles (nuclear radiation)


Radiation ≠ always harmful.

B. Non-Ionizing Radiation (Low Energy)
Examples:
Radio waves


Microwaves


Infrared


Visible light


UV-A & UV-B (low-energy ultraviolet)


Properties:
Cannot ionize atoms


Mostly harmless at normal levels


Everyday devices use this safely


Analogy: Like a gentle push — not enough force to knock anything over.

C. Ionizing Radiation (High Energy)
Types:
Alpha particles: heavy, low penetration


Beta particles: light, moderate penetration


Gamma rays: highly penetrating electromagnetic waves


Neutrons: deeply penetrating particles


Properties:
Has enough energy to remove electrons


Requires proper safety measures


Found in medical imaging, nuclear science, cosmic rays, natural sources


Analogy: Like a strong punch — powerful, but controllable with protection.

Lesson 8: Radiation Interaction & Biological Effects
Title: Radiation Interaction & Biological Effects
Description: Explain how radiation interacts with the human body, the difference between acute and chronic exposure, deterministic vs stochastic effects, and how cells repair radiation damage. Debunk exaggerated beliefs around radiation harm.
Unit: Unit 3 — Radiation & Its Effects
Difficulty: Intermediate
Prerequisite: Lesson 7 (Types of radiation)
Default Steps
1. BRIEFING - Mission Intro
“Agent, radiation doesn’t just hit the body — it interacts with cells in specific ways. Your mission: learn what radiation actually does inside the body and how our cells can repair most of the damage.”

2. MYTH BUSTER - Bust a misconception
Myth: “Nuclear radiation instantly causes mutations.”
Truth: Most radiation exposure causes no immediate effects.
Cells have repair mechanisms, and mutations only occur at high doses or prolonged exposure. Everyday radiation levels do not cause instant or extreme effects.

3. MICRO LEARN - Core teaching content
A. How Radiation Interacts with Matter
Ionizing radiation can:
Remove electrons → ionization


Break molecular bonds


Create free radicals


Analogy: Like bumping into a row of dominoes — possible to knock one, but not the whole line.
B. Types of Exposure
Acute exposure: High dose in a short time (e.g., radiation accident)


Chronic exposure: Low dose over long periods (background radiation, environment)


Most real-life exposure is chronic and low-dose — generally safe.

C. Biological Effects
There are two main categories:
1. Deterministic Effects (Have a Threshold)
Happen only if dose is high enough.
 Examples:
Skin burns


Acute Radiation Syndrome (ARS)


Organ damage


If the threshold is not reached → these effects DO NOT occur.

2. Stochastic Effects (No Threshold)
Probability increases with dose, but severity does not.
 Example:
Cancer risk (very small at low doses)


Analogy: Like raffle tickets — more tickets → higher chance, not bigger prize.

D. Cell Repair Mechanisms
Cells can:
Repair DNA


Replace damaged cells


Remove affected tissues


Most low-dose damage is repaired naturally.
E. Everyday Radiation Sources
Food


Cosmic rays


Soil & rocks


Medical imaging


Radon gas


Radiation is natural and has been part of life since Earth formed.

Lesson 9: Measuring Radiation & Safety Standards
Title: Measuring Radiation & Safety Standards
Description: Introduce radiation units (Becquerel, Gray, Sievert), detection tools (Geiger counter, dosimeters), and internationally recognized safety standards. Learners understand exposure limits, shielding, distance, and time principles.
Unit: Unit 3 — Radiation & Its Effects
Difficulty: Intermediate
Prerequisite:
Lesson 7 (Radiation types)
Lesson 8 (Biological effects)
Default Steps
1. BRIEFING - Mission Intro
“Agent, radiation can’t be seen — but with the right tools, you can measure it. Your mission: learn how scientists detect radiation and how safety standards protect people every day.”


2. MYTH BUSTER - Bust a misconception
Myth: “There is no safe level of radiation.”
Truth: We are exposed to natural radiation 24/7. International guidelines define safe limits, and most radiation levels in daily life fall far below harmful thresholds.


3. MICRO LEARN - Core teaching content

A. Radiation Units


Unit
Measures
Meaning
Becquerel (Bq)
Radioactivity
Rate of decay
Gray (Gy)
Absorbed dose
Energy absorbed per kg of tissue
Sievert (Sv)
Effective dose
Biological impact of radiation




Analogy:
Bq = how many bullets were fired


Gy = how many hit your armor


Sv = how much your body was affected

B. Radiation Measuring Devices
Geiger counter: Detects ionizing radiation levels instantly


Dosimeter: Worn to track accumulated exposure


Scintillation detectors: High-sensitivity gamma detection


Ionization chambers: Accurate measurement for labs/industry

C. Safety Principles
1. Time — minimize the duration of exposure
 2. Distance — increase distance to reduce dose
 3. Shielding — use materials that block radiation
Alpha → paper


Beta → plastic or aluminum


Gamma → lead or concrete


Neutrons → water or hydrogen-rich materials
D. Safety Standards
Set by:
IAEA (International Atomic Energy Agency)


WHO


UNSCEAR


Nuclear regulatory agencies


Standards ensure safety for:
Medical imaging


Industry


Research labs


Nuclear plants
